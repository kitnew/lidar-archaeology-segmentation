\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

Remote sensing has become one of the key sources of archaeological information, because it allows researchers to study large landscapes without relying exclusively on direct excavation or pedestrian survey. Among remote sensing technologies, airborne LiDAR has had a particularly strong impact on archaeological prospection, especially in forested or otherwise difficult terrain. By providing high-resolution terrain models with reduced vegetation influence, LiDAR-derived data make it possible to observe subtle micro-relief features such as mounds, platforms, terraces, embankments, roads, or other anthropogenic terrain modifications that may be difficult or impossible to identify from conventional optical imagery~\cite{thompson_detecting_2020,kokalj_standardizing_2025}.

At the same time, the increasing availability of LiDAR data has created a new methodological problem. The amount of terrain data that can be collected has grown faster than the capacity of experts to inspect, annotate, and verify it manually. Traditional archaeological interpretation still relies strongly on expert visual analysis of derived terrain visualizations, and this expert role remains essential. However, manual interpretation is time-consuming, difficult to scale to large areas, and sensitive to differences in experience, visualisation choice, annotation conventions, and local archaeological context~\cite{trier_using_2019,verschoof-van_der_vaart_combining_2020,kokalj_standardizing_2025}. Therefore, the main challenge is not to remove the expert from the workflow, but to develop computational methods that can support the expert by reducing repetitive search tasks and by making the processing workflow more systematic and reproducible.

Deep learning, and especially convolutional neural networks, provides a promising direction for this task. In the context of LiDAR archaeology, neural networks can be trained on raster representations derived from terrain data and can produce pixel-wise predictions indicating the likely presence of archaeological structures. Previous studies have already demonstrated the potential of U-Net, Mask R-CNN, YOLO-based methods, and other CNN architectures for archaeological object detection, classification, and segmentation in LiDAR-derived data~\cite{bundzel_semantic_2020,somrak_learning_2020,guyot_objective_2021,zhang_unveiling_2024}. These methods can process large areas much faster than manual interpretation and may help identify candidate structures that should be inspected or verified by archaeologists.

Despite these promising results, the use of deep learning for archaeological LiDAR segmentation remains methodologically challenging. Published studies often differ in data preparation, terrain visualization, label construction, model architecture, sampling strategy, loss function, threshold selection, and evaluation procedure. As a result, it is difficult to determine which components of the workflow have a substantial influence on the final prediction quality and which are only secondary implementation details. This lack of standardization limits the comparability of studies and complicates the transfer of methods between different regions, object types, and data resolutions~\cite{fiorucci_deep_2022,kokalj_standardizing_2025}.

Several specific problems are especially relevant for this thesis:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Choice of input representation.} LiDAR-derived terrain data can be represented through different raster products and visualizations, such as DEM, hillshade, slope, sky-view factor, openness, or combined multi-band visualizations. Each representation emphasizes different properties of the terrain surface. Consequently, the selected representation can influence which archaeological structures are visible to the model and how well they can be separated from the surrounding natural terrain~\cite{thompson_detecting_2020,kokalj_standardizing_2025}.

    \item \textbf{Quality and form of reference labels.} Archaeological annotations are usually produced for expert interpretation and documentation, not primarily for machine learning. Therefore, the label geometry may be incomplete, conservative, spatially imprecise, or inconsistent with the exact topographic expression visible in the raster data. For segmentation models, these properties directly affect the training signal and may lead to noisy or ambiguous supervision~\cite{bundzel_semantic_2020,rahaman_effects_2022,burgert_effects_2022}.

    \item \textbf{Extreme class imbalance.} Archaeological objects usually occupy only a very small fraction of all pixels in a LiDAR raster. The background class is therefore dominant, while the target class may be sparse, narrow, fragmented, or locally discontinuous. Without an appropriate sampling strategy and loss function, the model can learn to prefer the background class and suppress small archaeological features~\cite{diakogiannis_resunet-_2020,zhou_dynamic_2023}.

    \item \textbf{Evaluation of practical usefulness.} Standard pixel-wise metrics, such as Intersection over Union, precision, recall, or F1-score, are necessary for quantitative comparison, but they do not always fully reflect the archaeological value of a prediction. A segmentation result that is slightly shifted from the reference label may receive a low pixel-wise score, even though it can still be useful as a candidate for expert inspection or field verification. For this reason, quantitative evaluation should be complemented by qualitative and GIS-based assessment~\cite{fiorucci_deep_2022,guyot_objective_2021}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Motivation}

The motivation of this thesis follows from the gap between the potential of deep learning methods and the current methodological uncertainty in their application to archaeological LiDAR data. Existing research shows that neural networks can support archaeological prospection, but it also shows that the final result depends on many decisions made before, during, and after model training. These decisions include the choice of input representation, the transformation of expert annotations into training masks, the sampling of highly imbalanced data, the selection of a loss function, the optimization of the prediction threshold, and the final interpretation of the output in a GIS environment.

In practical terms, the problem is that a trained segmentation model is not useful only because it produces a numerical score. It is useful only if its predictions can help an archaeologist inspect large areas more efficiently, identify candidate features, and decide where further verification may be needed. Therefore, the main motivation is to build and evaluate a reproducible experimental workflow that makes these decisions explicit and comparable. Such a workflow can clarify which components improve segmentation quality, which configurations are unstable, and how the same methodology behaves in different archaeological and geographical contexts.

This thesis is motivated by two complementary data domains: Maya archaeological structures in Guatemala and archaeological terrace systems in Slovakia. These datasets differ in object morphology, spatial resolution, annotation type, and landscape context. Their comparison makes it possible to study not only whether semantic segmentation can be applied to archaeological LiDAR data, but also how sensitive the workflow is to changes in the data domain. This is important because a method that works only in one narrowly defined case has limited scientific and practical value.

\section{Formulation of the problem}

The problem addressed in this thesis is the design and experimental evaluation of a reproducible semantic segmentation workflow for archaeological objects in airborne LiDAR-derived raster data. The thesis focuses on two archaeological contexts: the segmentation of Maya-related structures in the Guatemalan dataset and the segmentation of terrace-like archaeological features in the Slovak dataset.

The task is formulated as a binary semantic segmentation problem, where each valid pixel of the input raster is classified either as archaeological structure or as background. Solving this task requires several connected steps: preparation of LiDAR-derived input representations, transformation of available expert annotations into training masks, construction of a tiled dataset suitable for deep learning, training of segmentation models under controlled experimental conditions, threshold-based generation of binary predictions, and evaluation of the results using both quantitative metrics and qualitative GIS-based inspection.

The aim of this thesis is therefore not to create a fully autonomous archaeological discovery system that would replace expert interpretation. Instead, the aim is to propose, implement, and evaluate a controlled deep learning workflow that can support archaeological prospection by producing reproducible segmentation outputs and by clarifying the influence of selected methodological components on the final result. The expected outcome is an experimentally verified assessment of how different data representations, label forms, sampling strategies, loss functions, and evaluation procedures affect the segmentation of archaeological features in LiDAR-derived data.
