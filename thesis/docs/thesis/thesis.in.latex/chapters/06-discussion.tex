\chapter{Results and Discussion}\label{ch:results_discussion}

This chapter presents the quantitative and qualitative results of the conducted experiments. The experiments were treated not as an independent sequence of model runs, but as a set of controlled experimental groups. Each group was designed to assess the impact of a single methodological component, such as input data representation, label representation, \gls{lossfunction}, sampling strategy, or model architecture. This structure allows us to distinguish general trends from the specific results of individual configurations. A detailed description of all configurations will not be provided due to their high variability; they follow directly from the methodology described above and adhere to a defined evaluation protocol.

Experiments were conducted separately for the datasets on \gls{mayastructure} and \gls{slovakterrace}, as these two study areas differ in object morphology, terrain context, and annotation characteristics. The Maya dataset focuses primarily on compact archaeological structures, whereas the Slovak dataset contains linear objects similar to terraces. Therefore, the results are presented separately for both areas and only then compared at the level of methodological trends. A complete description of the characteristics of the available data is provided in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

Only experiments conducted on the latest consistent version of the workflow will be considered to ensure the objectivity of the subsequent comparison and to minimize the impact of possible methodological drift from minor changes. This will ensure a meaningful comparison of the tested components regardless of the input data domain.

In this context, the main goal of improving the stability of the results is not to segment existing structures while preserving the correct geometry, but rather to ensure that no potential candidate objects are overlooked that might be of interest in an expert review.

\section{Reference Configurations and Main Results}

This section presents the reference configurations selected for detailed comparison in the results chapter. For each study domain, the best-performing configuration is first described in terms of model architecture, \gls{inputrepresentation}, label representation, \gls{lossfunction}, and \gls{tile} configuration. The reported results are then compared with a representative baseline in order to show how the final configuration differs from earlier or simpler experimental variants.

\subsection{Best-Performing Configuration for Maya Structures}

For the Maya dataset, the best-performing configuration was obtained with the following setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Model: DeepLab V3+ with an ImageNet-pretrained \gls{resnet}-101 \gls{backbone};
    \item Input representation: z-score-normalized \gls{sps} representation;
    \item Label representation: soft mask with a 2~m \gls{confidenceshadow} radius;
    \item Loss function: Tanimoto with complement loss;
    \item \gls{tile} size: $128 \times 128$ pixels with a \gls{stride} of 32 pixels.
\end{itemize}

This configuration is used as the reference result for the \gls{mayastructure} because it achieved the strongest overall performance among the evaluated variants. To provide a meaningful comparison, it is contrasted with the older DeepLab V3 model trained without pretrained weights. This comparison should not be interpreted as an isolated evaluation of pretraining alone, since the models also differ architecturally. However, it remains useful as a representative comparison between the strongest final configuration and the earlier baseline, especially because the non-pretrained DeepLab V3 and DeepLab V3+ variants showed only minor differences in the preliminary experiments.

The pixel-wise results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:maya_v3_v3plus_metrics}. Only the metrics that provide useful information for this highly imbalanced segmentation problem are reported. Overall accuracy and background-dominated measures were omitted, since they can overestimate performance when the \gls{foregroundclass} occupies only a small part of the raster. The results show that the DeepLab V3+ configuration improves all retained \gls{pixelwisemetric} indicators. The most important improvements are visible in foreground \gls{iou}, \gls{fscore}, and Matthews correlation coefficient, which indicates that the model provides a more balanced segmentation of the archaeological structures rather than merely preserving the dominant \gls{backgroundclass}.

Since \gls{pixelwisemetric} do not fully describe the practical usefulness of the predictions in archaeological prospection, an object-wise evaluation was also performed. The results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:maya_v3_v3plus_object_metrics}. From the \gls{objectwisemetric} perspective, DeepLab V3+ detected 2253 ground-truth objects, compared with 1746 objects detected by DeepLab V3. Given the total number of 4084 reference objects, this corresponds to a substantially higher coverage of the available archaeological structures. At the same time, the average pixel \gls{hitrate} per detected object is slightly lower for DeepLab V3+, and the number of \gls{falsepositive} predicted objects is higher. Therefore, the improvement of DeepLab V3+ should be interpreted primarily as an increase in detection completeness rather than as a reduction of all error types.

This behaviour is consistent with the practical objective of the task. In the context of archaeological \gls{candidatedetection}, missing a potential structure may be more problematic than producing additional candidates that can later be reviewed by an expert~\cite{guyot_combined_2021,verschoof-van_der_vaart_combining_2020,somrak_learning_2020,bundzel_semantic_2020}. The qualitative comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:maya_tp_fp_fn_comparison} supports this interpretation. Both models tend to fail in similar situations, but the comparison also shows several important qualitative differences. In panel (a), most errors occur near correctly segmented structures: \gls{falsenegative} pixels are often located directly next to true-positive regions, which indicates boundary mismatch rather than a complete failure to localize the object. In panel (b), the missed structures are located in areas where the terrain expression is weaker and the object morphology is less clearly separated from the surrounding background. In this case, the DeepLab V3+ configuration detects one additional structure compared with DeepLab V3. Panel (c) illustrates \gls{falsepositive}, where the models activate on terrain forms that visually resemble archaeological structures but are not included in the \gls{referenceannotation}. Overall, the DeepLab V3+ configuration identifies a larger portion of potential structures and therefore provides a more useful basis for subsequent expert-guided interpretation.

\subsection{Best-Performing Configuration for Slovak Terraces}

For the \gls{slovakterrace} dataset, the best-performing configuration was obtained with the following setup:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Model: DeepLab V3+ with a \gls{resnet}-101 \gls{backbone};
    \item Input representation: \gls{aniso} visualization;
    \item Label representation: soft mask with a 2~m \gls{confidenceshadow} radius;
    \item Loss function: \gls{bcediceloss};
    \item \gls{tile} size: $512 \times 512$ pixels with a \gls{stride} of 128 pixels.
\end{itemize}

This configuration is used as the reference result for the \gls{slovakterrace} dataset because it achieved the strongest overall performance among the evaluated variants. However, in contrast to the \gls{mayastructure}, the interpretation of the Slovak results is less straightforward. The terrace task involves narrow, elongated, and partially discontinuous features represented by \gls{buffer} polylines. Therefore, a prediction may correctly indicate the presence of a terrace complex while still obtaining relatively low pixel-wise overlap with the \gls{referencemask}.

To keep the comparison consistent with the Maya results, the selected DeepLab V3+ configuration is compared with the same architecture using an ImageNet-pretrained \gls{backbone}. The comparison shows a clear difference between the two variants. Although the pretrained model showed more stable behaviour during training, the final validation metrics favour the model trained from scratch. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:slovakia_v3_v3plus_metrics}, the non-pretrained DeepLab V3+ achieved higher balanced accuracy, \gls{truepositive} rate, \gls{fscore}, foreground \gls{iou}, average \gls{iou}, and Matthews correlation coefficient. The only retained \gls{pixelwisemetric} metric in which the pretrained model performed better is positive predictive value, indicating a more conservative prediction behaviour.

The non-pretrained DeepLab V3+ detected more ground-truth terrace objects and produced a substantially higher average \gls{hitrate} per detected object. At the same time, it generated more predicted objects and a larger number of \gls{falsepositive}. The ImageNet-pretrained model produced fewer \gls{falsepositive} objects and more true-positive predicted components, but it detected fewer ground-truth objects overall. This suggests that the pretrained model is more selective, whereas the non-pretrained model is more sensitive to terrace-like terrain patterns.

In both cases, the model responds to broader terrace-like morphology rather than to individual \gls{buffer} terrace lines. This behaviour explains the relatively low pixel-wise overlap and the high number of \gls{falsepositive}.

Overall, the \gls{slovakterrace} results should therefore be interpreted more cautiously than the Maya results. The selected configuration is the most useful among the tested variants because it provides the highest coverage of potential terrace areas and the strongest foreground-oriented metrics. Nevertheless, the high number of \gls{falsepositive} and the merging of individual terraces show that the model output is not suitable as a final segmentation map. Its practical value is closer to a candidate layer for expert inspection, where the model can highlight possible terrace complexes but still requires manual verification and interpretation.

\section{Ablation Study}

The purpose of the \gls{ablationstudy} is to evaluate the influence of individual methodological components on the final segmentation performance. Each experiment group isolates one controlled variable, while the remaining components are kept identical within that group. Therefore, the comparisons below should be interpreted only inside the corresponding group: \gls{inputrepresentation} is compared with fixed training settings, label representation is compared within paired configurations, \gls{tile} size is compared with the same model and loss setup, and so forth. This makes it possible to attribute the observed differences to the tested component rather than to unrelated changes in the pipeline.

\subsection{Effect of Input Representation}

The first ablation group evaluates the effect of \gls{lidarderiveddata} \gls{inputrepresentation}. Four representations were compared on the Maya dataset: \gls{dem}, \gls{rgb}, \gls{rgdem}, and \gls{sps}. All variants were evaluated using the same model architecture, label representation, \gls{lossfunction}, \gls{tile} configuration, training procedure, validation split, and threshold optimization strategy. Therefore, the \gls{inputrepresentation} is the only controlled component in this comparison.

The results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:maya_input_metrics}. Among the tested representations, \gls{sps} achieved the strongest performance across all reported metrics. The foreground \gls{iou} increased to 0.43, compared with 0.37 for \gls{rgdem}, 0.36 for \gls{rgb}, and 0.25 for \gls{dem}. The same trend is visible in \gls{precision}, \gls{recall}, and \gls{fscore}. This indicates that the combination of \gls{slope}, \gls{positiveopenness}, and \gls{skyviewfactor} provides more informative local terrain contrast than the direct \gls{dem} representation or \gls{rgb}-like visualization alone.

The \gls{dem} representation produced the weakest result. This is expected, since absolute elevation values do not necessarily emphasize local micro-relief patterns associated with archaeological structures~\cite{kokalj_standardizing_2025}. In contrast, \gls{sps} explicitly encodes local terrain morphology and therefore makes subtle anthropogenic features more separable from the natural background. \gls{rgdem} and \gls{rgb} performed similarly, with \gls{rgdem} achieving slightly better foreground \gls{iou} and \gls{fscore}. However, the improvement remains smaller than the difference between \gls{sps} and the other input variants.

\subsection{Effect of Label Representation}

The second ablation group evaluates the effect of label representation. Hard binary masks are compared with soft masks based on the same \gls{inputrepresentation}. In this table, the relevant comparison is therefore not between all columns globally, but between each original representation and its corresponding soft-mask variant. All other components, including model architecture, \gls{inputrepresentation}, \gls{lossfunction}, \gls{tile} size, and validation protocol, are kept unchanged inside each pair.

The results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:maya_label_metrics}. The effect of \gls{softlabel} is not uniform. For \gls{sps} and \gls{rgb}, soft masks slightly improved foreground \gls{iou} and \gls{fscore}. For \gls{rgdem}, the soft-mask variant produced a minor decrease in foreground \gls{iou} and \gls{fscore}. For \gls{dem}, the difference is negligible. This indicates that soft boundary supervision does not automatically improve segmentation quality in all cases.

The most consistent effect of soft masks is visible in the \gls{precision}--\gls{recall} balance. In most paired comparisons, soft masks reduce \gls{precision} and increase \gls{recall}. Such behaviour may be useful when the objective is to avoid missing archaeological candidates, but it also increases the risk of \gls{falsepositive}. Therefore, \gls{softlabel} should be interpreted as a trade-off between more inclusive detection and lower prediction selectivity.

\subsection{Effect of Tile Size}

The third ablation group evaluates the effect of \gls{tile} size. In this comparison, the model architecture, \gls{inputrepresentation}, label representation, \gls{lossfunction}, training procedure, validation split, and threshold optimization strategy are kept fixed. Only the \gls{tile} size and corresponding \gls{stride} are changed.

For the Maya dataset, the \gls{tile} dimensions can be interpreted directly in metres because the raster resolution is 1 m per pixel. The comparison is shown in Table~\ref{tab:maya_tile_metrics}. The smaller $128 \times 128$ m \gls{tile} with a \gls{stride} of 32 m outperformed the $256 \times 256$ m \gls{tile} with a \gls{stride} of 64 m across all reported metrics. The improvement is especially visible in foreground \gls{iou} and \gls{precision}. This suggests that, for compact \gls{mayastructure}, the smaller \gls{tile} size provides sufficient context while preserving a higher relative density of positive pixels inside the training samples.

\subsection{Effect of Loss Function}

The fourth ablation group evaluates the influence of the \gls{lossfunction}. All compared variants use the same model architecture, \gls{inputrepresentation}, label representation, \gls{tile} size, training procedure, validation split, and threshold optimization strategy. Therefore, the differences in Table~\ref{tab:maya_loss_metrics} reflect the effect of the optimization objective.

\begin{table}[H]

    \label{tab:maya_loss_metrics}

The observed training curves showed that \gls{bce}+Dice was more prone to unstable validation behaviour and overfitting, whereas \gls{tanimotoloss} produced smoother and more stable convergence. Therefore, \gls{bce}+Dice can be treated as the best metric-oriented loss in this group, while Tanimoto remains a strong alternative when training stability is prioritized.

\section{Error Analysis}

The error analysis was conducted to identify the main failure modes of the trained segmentation models and to relate them to the properties of archaeological \gls{lidar} data. The analysis is based on qualitative inspection of selected prediction maps in a \gls{gis} environment. The examples distinguish between \gls{falsepositive}, \gls{falsenegative}, \glspl{truepositive}, and boundary-related errors. This qualitative step is necessary because \gls{pixelwisemetric} alone do not fully explain whether a prediction is archaeologically meaningful or merely numerically correct.

The observed errors can be divided into three main categories: \gls{falsepositive}, \gls{falsenegative}, and boundary errors. These categories are closely related to the morphology of archaeological objects, the visual similarity between anthropogenic and natural terrain forms, and the uncertainty of expert annotations.

False-positive predictions occur when the model identifies terrain forms as archaeological structures even though they are not included in the \gls{referenceannotation}. An example is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:maya_fp_example_1}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/maya_fp_example_1.pdf}
    \caption{Example of \gls{falsepositive} on the Maya dataset. The model activates on terrain forms that are not included in the \gls{referenceannotation} but visually resemble archaeological structures.}
    \label{fig:maya_fp_example_1}
\end{figure}

The predicted regions are located on local relief anomalies that visually resemble archaeological remains. These forms include curved, rectangular, or edge-like terrain patterns with strong local contrast. From the model perspective, such areas are difficult to distinguish from actual structures because they contain similar geometric and topographic cues. Therefore, \gls{falsepositive} are not random noise, but usually appear in places where the terrain morphology is similar to the learned archaeological class.

Several sources of \gls{falsepositive} were identified. The first source is natural terrain morphology that resembles anthropogenic structures, especially small mounds, depressions, sharp \gls{slope} transitions, or locally regular relief forms. The second source is modern disturbance, such as tracks, edges of modified terrain, or other recent landscape changes. The third source is the visualization itself: \gls{lidarderiveddata} representations may enhance local contrast and create artefacts that appear structurally meaningful to the model~\cite{kokalj_standardizing_2025}. In addition, some predictions classified as \gls{falsepositive} may represent unannotated or ambiguous candidates rather than clear model errors, since the available labels should not be interpreted as a perfect archaeological inventory.

False-negative errors occur when annotated archaeological objects are missed by the model. Figure~\ref{fig:maya_fn_example} shows that this problem is especially common for small, isolated, weakly preserved, or low-contrast structures.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/maya_fn_example.pdf}
    \caption{Examples of \gls{falsenegative} on the Maya dataset. Blue denotes \gls{falsenegative}, red denotes \gls{falsepositive}, and white denotes true-positive overlap. Panels (a1--a3) show small, isolated, or weakly expressed structures that are mostly missed by the model. Panels (b1--b3) show larger or more coherent structure groups, where some parts are detected but many neighbouring annotated structures remain \gls{falsenegative}.}
    \label{fig:maya_fn_example}
\end{figure}

In many cases, the objects are visible in the \gls{referenceannotation} but only weakly expressed in the \gls{terrainvisualization}. Such objects provide limited visual evidence for the model and can easily be suppressed as background, particularly when they are surrounded by heterogeneous natural terrain.

The \gls{falsenegative} examples also show that the model performs better on larger or more coherent groups of structures than on individual small objects. In the lower part of Figure~\ref{fig:maya_fn_example}, several structures are partially detected, but the segmentation remains incomplete. This indicates that the model can recognize stronger archaeological morphology, but it often fails to recover the complete set of small neighbouring structures. For archaeological prospection, this is an important limitation because small structures form a substantial part of the reference dataset and may be relevant for settlement interpretation.

Boundary errors represent a separate class of problems. They occur when the model detects the correct archaeological object or terrain feature, but its predicted extent does not match the \gls{referencemask}. Figure~\ref{fig:maya_boundaries_error} illustrates a strong boundary mismatch.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/maya_boundaries_error.pdf}
    \caption{Example of a strong boundary mismatch on the Maya dataset. Panel (a) shows the \gls{lidarderiveddata} \gls{terrainvisualization}, while panel (b) shows the evaluation overlay, where blue denotes \gls{falsenegative}, red denotes \gls{falsepositive}, and white denotes true-positive overlap. The model detects only the most visually expressed parts of the structure, while a large part of the annotated area remains \gls{falsenegative}.}
    \label{fig:maya_boundaries_error}
\end{figure}

The model identifies only parts of the visible relief expression, while a large part of the annotated area remains classified as \gls{falsenegative}. This suggests that the model follows the present-day terrain signal rather than the full idealized or expert-interpreted outline stored in the \gls{referenceannotation}.

This behaviour is expected in archaeological \gls{lidar} segmentation. Expert annotations often represent interpreted archaeological objects rather than exact present-day relief boundaries~\cite{bundzel_semantic_2020,guyot_combined_2021,fiorucci_deep_2022,somrak_learning_2020}. As a result, a structure may be annotated as a coherent polygon, while the visible terrain expression is fragmented, eroded, or shifted. The model, however, learns directly from raster appearance. It therefore tends to activate on the most visually expressed parts of the object, such as edges, mounds, walls, or \gls{slope} transitions, instead of reproducing the full archaeological outline.

A contrasting example is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:maya_boundaries_success}. In this case, the model successfully identifies the main structure and produces a spatially coherent prediction.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/maya_boundaries_success.pdf}
    \caption{Example of a successful but not perfect boundary prediction on the Maya dataset. Panel (a) shows the \gls{lidarderiveddata} \gls{terrainvisualization}, while panel (b) shows the evaluation overlay, where blue denotes \gls{falsenegative}, red denotes \gls{falsepositive}, and white denotes true-positive overlap. The main archaeological structure is correctly localized as a coherent prediction, although minor boundary disagreement remains along the object edges.}
    \label{fig:maya_boundaries_success}
\end{figure}

Nevertheless, small red and blue regions remain along the object boundary. This demonstrates that even successful predictions often contain minor boundary disagreement. Such errors may reduce \gls{pixelwisemetric}, although the result remains useful for expert inspection because the main archaeological object is correctly localized.

Soft masks partially address this problem by reducing the penalty for small boundary deviations. However, they do not eliminate the underlying mismatch between the raster-visible relief and the expert-defined archaeological outline. Moreover, softer supervision can encourage broader predictions and increase \gls{falsepositive}. Therefore, boundary errors should be interpreted not only as model inaccuracies, but also as evidence of uncertainty in the relation between annotation geometry and terrain expression.

Overall, the error analysis shows that the model is more reliable at identifying distinct and morphologically clear archaeological structures than at precisely delineating all reference boundaries. The main practical limitation is not only the presence of \gls{falsepositive} or \gls{falsenegative}, but the ambiguity of the target class itself. Archaeological structures are often eroded, partially preserved, visually similar to natural forms, and represented by annotations that may be schematic or conservative. For this reason, the model output should be treated as a candidate layer for expert-guided interpretation rather than as a final archaeological map.

\section{Archaeological Usefulness of the Results}

The obtained results should not be interpreted as a replacement for expert archaeological interpretation. The models developed in this thesis are more appropriate as decision-support tools that highlight candidate areas for further inspection. This interpretation is especially important in archaeological \gls{lidar} analysis, where the main practical objective is often not the automatic production of a final archaeological inventory, but the reduction of the area that must be manually inspected by an expert.

From this perspective, \gls{falsepositive} and \gls{falsenegative} do not have the same practical impact. A \gls{falsepositive} prediction increases the amount of material that must be reviewed, but it can often be rejected relatively quickly during expert inspection. A \gls{falsenegative} is more problematic, because it represents a potentially missed \gls{archaeologicalfeature}~\cite{guyot_combined_2021,verschoof-van_der_vaart_combining_2020,somrak_learning_2020}. Therefore, in the context of exploratory prospection, a model with higher candidate coverage may still be useful even if it produces additional \gls{falsepositive}.

For the Maya dataset, the model output is particularly useful for candidate structure detection. The \gls{objectwisemetric} \glspl{hitrate} by size category are shown in Table~\ref{tab:maya_hit_rate_by_size}. The results demonstrate a strong dependence on object size. Large structures were detected most reliably, with 400 detected objects out of 472, corresponding to a \gls{hitrate} of 0.8475. Medium-sized structures were also detected relatively well, with 1383 detected objects out of 2182 and a \gls{hitrate} of 0.6338. In contrast, small structures were considerably more difficult to detect, with only 470 detected objects out of 1430 and a \gls{hitrate} of 0.3287. In both cases, the output should be treated as a supporting layer for \gls{expertguidedinspection} rather than as a final archaeological interpretation.

\section{Limitations}

Several limitations must be considered when interpreting the results of this thesis. These limitations are related not only to the trained models themselves, but also to the available reference data, the morphology of the archaeological objects, the evaluation protocol, and the practical use of the predicted masks in a \gls{gis} environment.

The first limitation concerns the uncertainty of the reference data. The available annotations should not be interpreted as perfect \gls{groundtruth}. In the Maya dataset, the labels represent expert-interpreted archaeological structures and may include simplified or idealized boundaries. In the Slovak dataset, the terrace annotations are represented as \gls{buffer} polylines rather than precise polygonal outlines of the actual relief expression. As a result, there may be a spatial mismatch between the visible terrain morphology and the \gls{referencemask} used for training and evaluation. This limitation directly affects both model learning and quantitative metrics, because the model may detect a real terrain feature but still be penalized if the prediction does not coincide with the annotation geometry.

The second limitation is the strong \gls{classimbalance} between \gls{archaeologicalfeature} and background. In both datasets, the target class occupies only a small fraction of the raster area. This imbalance affects training, threshold selection, and metric interpretation. A model can achieve high background performance while still failing to detect a substantial part of the archaeological class. For this reason, overall accuracy and background-dominated measures are not sufficient for evaluating the results. Even foreground-oriented metrics such as \gls{iou}, \gls{precision}, \gls{recall}, and \gls{fscore} must be interpreted together with qualitative inspection.

The third limitation is the limited cross-\gls{domaingeneralization} between the Maya and Slovak datasets. Although both datasets are based on airborne \gls{lidarderiveddata} terrain data, they differ substantially in spatial resolution, object morphology, annotation type, and landscape context. The Maya dataset contains mostly compact polygonal structures, while the Slovak dataset contains narrow, elongated, and partially discontinuous terrace-like features. Therefore, a configuration that performs well on the \gls{mayastructure} does not necessarily transfer directly to \gls{slovakterrace}. The results show that each domain requires its own adjustment of \gls{inputrepresentation}, \gls{tile} size, \gls{lossfunction}, and interpretation strategy.

A related limitation is the difference in object morphology. Compact \gls{mayastructure} are more compatible with standard \gls{semanticsegmentation} assumptions, because their spatial extent is relatively coherent and object boundaries are often easier to localize. In contrast, \gls{slovakterrace} are linear features with weak relief expression, fragmented continuity, and strong dependence on surrounding \gls{slope} morphology. This makes them more difficult to segment as continuous binary masks. In many cases, the model identifies a broader terrace complex rather than individual terrace lines. Therefore, the Slovak results should be interpreted primarily as candidate localization, not as precise delineation.

Another limitation is that \gls{pixelwisemetric} metrics do not fully capture archaeological usefulness. A prediction can be slightly shifted from the \gls{referencemask} and receive a low pixel-wise score, even though it correctly indicates a relevant archaeological candidate~\cite{fiorucci_deep_2022}. Conversely, a prediction can overlap with the \gls{referencemask} but still be fragmented or difficult to interpret archaeologically. This limitation is especially important for archaeological \gls{lidar} data, where labels may be uncertain and the practical goal is often to support expert inspection rather than produce a final automatic map. For this reason, the quantitative evaluation was complemented by qualitative \gls{gis}-based assessment.

Full-area inference also remains dependent on expert interpretation. The generated \gls{probabilitymap} and binary masks can reduce the amount of terrain that must be manually inspected, but they do not remove the need for archaeological expertise. False positives caused by natural relief forms, modern disturbances, visualization artefacts, or \gls{slope} edges must still be reviewed manually. Similarly, \gls{falsenegative} may occur in weakly preserved, small, or low-contrast structures. The model output should therefore be treated as a decision-support layer rather than as an independent archaeological inventory.

The final limitation concerns the model and architecture design. This thesis evaluates selected DeepLab-based \gls{semanticsegmentation} models under a controlled experimental workflow, but it does not exhaustively test all possible architectures or parameter settings. Further improvement may require domain-specific adjustment of architectural parameters, such as input resolution, receptive field size, dilation rates, decoder design, or feature fusion strategy. If object detection or instance segmentation models are used in future work, additional parameters such as anchor sizes, feature pyramid settings, or instance-mask resolution may also need to be adapted to the physical scale and morphology of archaeological objects.

Overall, these limitations do not invalidate the obtained results, but they define the scope within which the results should be interpreted. The developed workflow demonstrates that deep learning can support archaeological \gls{lidar} interpretation, especially by highlighting candidate areas. However, the results also show that reliable archaeological application requires careful data preparation, domain-specific validation, and expert-guided interpretation of model outputs.
