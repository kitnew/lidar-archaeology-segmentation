\chapter{Conclusion}\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary of the Work}

This bachelor thesis addressed the problem of binary semantic segmentation of archaeological features in airborne \gls{lidar}-derived raster data. The work was motivated by the need to support expert interpretation of large \gls{lidar} datasets, where manual inspection remains time-consuming and difficult to scale. The thesis focused on two different archaeological and geographical domains: compact Maya archaeological structures in Guatemala and terrace-like archaeological features in Slovakia. These domains differ in object morphology, annotation type, spatial context, and practical interpretation, which made it possible to evaluate not only the performance of selected segmentation models, but also the sensitivity of the complete workflow to domain-specific conditions.

The main objective of the thesis was fulfilled. A reproducible deep learning workflow for binary semantic segmentation of archaeological objects and terrain features in airborne \gls{lidar}-derived raster data was designed, implemented, and experimentally evaluated. The workflow covered the preparation of \gls{lidar}-derived data input representation, construction of hard and soft reference masks, generation of tile datasets, controlled train-validation splitting, model training, \gls{thresholdbasedinference}, quantitative evaluation, and qualitative \gls{gis}-based inspection. The implemented pipeline was applied to both study domains and allowed the comparison of several methodological components under consistent experimental conditions.

The partial objectives were also fulfilled. The analytical part of the thesis reviewed traditional remote sensing, \gls{lidar}-based archaeological prospection, semi-automatic methods, and deep learning approaches relevant to archaeological object detection and \gls{semanticsegmentation}. The methodological part prepared several input representations for \gls{cnn}-based segmentation, including \gls{dem}-based, RGB-like, hybrid, and derivative-based representations. Reference masks were prepared for both domains, and hard binary labels were compared with soft-label variants based on \gls{confidenceshadow}-like boundary representation. A tile dataset pipeline based on \gls{datasetmanifest}, \gls{validpixelmask}, and controlled spatial splits was constructed and used consistently across the final experiments. Selected DeepLab-based segmentation models were trained and compared on the Maya and Slovak datasets, and the influence of input representation, label representation, tile size, loss function, model architecture, thresholding, and post-processing was evaluated using both pixel-wise metrics and \gls{gis}-based qualitative inspection.

The work therefore did not only demonstrate that \gls{semanticsegmentation} can be applied to archaeological \gls{lidar}-derived data. More importantly, it showed that the usefulness of the result depends on a chain of methodological decisions. The experiments confirmed that input representation, label construction, tile size, loss function, and model architecture can substantially influence the final prediction. This is especially important in archaeological applications, where model outputs should support expert interpretation rather than replace it.

\section{Main Results and Contributions}

For the Maya dataset, the best-performing configuration was based on DeepLab~V3+ with an ImageNet-pretrained \gls{resnet}-101 backbone, z-score-normalized \gls{sps} input representation, soft labels with a radius of 2~m, Tanimoto with complement loss, and $128 \times 128$ pixel tile with a stride of 32 pixels. Compared with the earlier DeepLab~V3 baseline, this configuration improved the retained foreground-oriented pixel-wise metrics. The foreground \gls{iou} increased from 0.24690 to 0.37924, the \gls{f1} from 0.39602 to 0.54992, and the Matthews correlation coefficient from 0.39634 to 0.56307. At the object level, the selected DeepLab~V3+ configuration detected 2253 out of 4084 reference objects, while the baseline detected 1746 objects. This improvement should be interpreted mainly as an increase in detection completeness, not as a complete solution to all segmentation errors.

The object-wise analysis showed that the Maya model was most useful for medium and large structures. Large structures were detected most reliably, with 400 detected objects out of 472 and a hit rate of 0.8475. Medium-sized structures reached a hit rate of 0.6338, while small structures reached only 0.3287. This confirms that object size, preservation, and terrain expression strongly influence detectability. The model can therefore be considered useful as a candidate detector for expert-guided inspection, especially when the target structures have a clear and coherent terrain expression.

For the Slovak terrace dataset, the best-performing configuration was based on DeepLab~V3+ with a \gls{resnet}-101 backbone, \gls{aniso} input representation, soft labels with a radius of 2~m, BCE+Dice loss, and $512 \times 512$ pixel tile with a stride of 128 pixels. This configuration achieved the strongest overall result among the evaluated Slovak variants, but the interpretation of the output was more limited than in the Maya domain. The obtained foreground \gls{iou} of 0.06560 and \gls{f1} of 0.12313 show that the prediction is not suitable as a final segmentation map of terrace geometry. At the same time, the model detected 16 out of 24 reference terrace objects at the object level, including all three large objects and 13 out of 21 medium objects. This indicates that the model can still highlight probable terrace-like zones, even when precise pixel-wise overlap remains weak.

The difference between the Maya and Slovak results is one of the central findings of the thesis. Compact Maya structures are more compatible with standard \gls{semanticsegmentation} assumptions, because they form relatively coherent objects with more localized boundaries. Slovak terrace are narrow, elongated, partially discontinuous, and represented by \gls{buffer} polylines rather than precise polygonal outlines of the visible relief. As a result, the Slovak model often responded to broader terrace-like morphology rather than to individual terrace lines. This shows that a workflow that performs well in one archaeological domain cannot be transferred directly to another without domain-specific adjustment.

The ablation study confirmed the importance of \gls{lidar}-derived data input representation. On the Maya dataset, \gls{sps} achieved the strongest performance among the tested representations, while \gls{dem} produced the weakest result. This shows that direct elevation values alone were less informative for this task than representations emphasizing local terrain morphology. For the Slovak dataset, the best-performing configuration used \gls{aniso}, which further supports the conclusion that the optimal representation depends on the morphology of the target objects and the surrounding landscape context.

The comparison of hard- and soft-label representations showed that soft labels are useful, but not uniformly superior. They can make the model more inclusive and increase recall, which is valuable when the goal is to avoid missing potential archaeological candidates. However, this behaviour can also reduce precision and increase \gls{falsepositive}. This result is important because archaeological annotations are often uncertain, simplified, or spatially imprecise. Soft labels can partially represent this uncertainty, but they do not remove the need for careful interpretation of prediction boundaries.

The tile-size experiments showed that smaller tiles were more suitable for compact Maya structures, while larger tiles were more appropriate for Slovak terraces, where broader terrain context is needed. The loss-function comparison showed that \gls{bce}+Dice produced the strongest metric-oriented result in one comparison group, while Tanimoto with complement provided stable training behaviour and was used in the strongest Maya configuration. The architecture comparison showed that DeepLab~V3+ was preferable to DeepLab~V3 in the evaluated setting because it provided a better precision-recall balance and more stable practical behaviour.

The qualitative error analysis showed that the trained models did not fail randomly. False-positive predictions usually appeared on natural or disturbed terrain forms that visually resembled archaeological structures. False-negative predictions were especially common for small, isolated, weakly preserved, or low-contrast objects. Boundary errors occurred when the model localized a relevant structure but did not reproduce the full reference outline. These errors reflect a fundamental difficulty of archaeological \gls{lidar} segmentation: expert annotations do not always correspond exactly to the terrain expression visible in the raster representation. The model tends to follow the visible relief signal, while the annotation may represent an interpreted archaeological object. Therefore, part of the metric penalty reflects not only model error, but also uncertainty in the relation between the visible terrain morphology and the reference geometry.

The main contribution of the thesis is the design and evaluation of a reproducible workflow for archaeological \gls{lidar} segmentation. The work connected data preparation, label construction, tile dataset generation, model training, \gls{thresholdbasedinference}, quantitative evaluation, and \gls{gis}-based interpretation into one controlled experimental pipeline. A further contribution is the comparison of methodological components under consistent conditions. The thesis did not treat the trained model as a black-box result, but evaluated how selected decisions influenced the final output. The comparison of two different archaeological domains also demonstrated that deep learning workflows for archaeological prospection require domain-specific validation.

The practical contribution of the work lies in a realistic interpretation of deep learning in archaeological \gls{lidar} analysis. The obtained results are not sufficient for fully automatic archaeological mapping and should not be interpreted as a replacement for expert interpretation. They are, however, useful as decision-support outputs. In the Maya domain, the model can help identify candidate structures, especially medium and large ones. In the Slovak domain, the model can highlight probable terrace-like zones, although it cannot extract reliable terrace geometry automatically. In both cases, the prediction should be treated as a supporting \gls{gis} layer for expert-guided inspection and prioritization.

\section{Open Questions and Future Work}

Several open questions remain beyond the scope of this thesis. The most important one concerns the quality and structure of reference data. The experiments showed that annotations strongly influence both model training and evaluation. Future work should therefore focus on producing more consistent expert annotations, documenting label uncertainty, and distinguishing between visible terrain expression and interpreted archaeological extent. For the Slovak terrace dataset, more precise polygonal annotations or alternative label representations could help determine whether the main limitation comes from weak relief expression, unsuitable model design, or mismatch between \gls{buffer} polylines and actual terrace morphology.

Another important direction is the improvement of evaluation methodology. Pixel-wise metrics are necessary for controlled comparison, but they do not fully capture archaeological usefulness. A prediction can be slightly shifted from the reference mask and receive a low pixel-wise score while still indicating a relevant archaeological candidate. Conversely, a prediction can overlap the reference mask but remain fragmented or difficult to interpret in practice. Future work should therefore further develop object-wise metrics and \gls{gis}-based evaluation methods that consider candidate localization, distance from reference features, object size, connected components, and expert review categories.

The results also suggest the need for more domain-specific model design. This thesis evaluated selected DeepLab-based \gls{semanticsegmentation} architectures, but archaeological \gls{lidar} data may benefit from models better adapted to the physical scale and morphology of the target objects. For compact Maya structures, architectures preserving local boundary detail may improve delineation. For Slovak terraces, models designed for narrow, elongated, and discontinuous structures may be more appropriate. Future experiments may therefore investigate stronger multiscale feature fusion, higher-resolution decoders, attention mechanisms, line-aware segmentation methods, or architectures specifically designed for thin objects.

Further work should also compare binary semantic segmentation with object detection and instance segmentation approaches. The results of this thesis show that object-wise usefulness can be more important than exact pixel-wise overlap. For Maya structures, instance-level methods could help separate individual structures. For Slovak terrace, object detection or line-aware methods may be more suitable if the practical goal is candidate localization rather than precise mask extraction.

Class imbalance and \gls{falsepositive} control remain open problems. Archaeological features occupy only a small fraction of the raster area, which affects training, threshold selection, and interpretation of full-area inference. Future research should further investigate sampling strategies, loss function, thresholding procedures, and post-processing methods that improve recall without producing an excessive number of \gls{falsepositive} candidates. This is especially important for practical \gls{gis} use, where a large number of \gls{falsepositive} can reduce the value of the model output.

Broader cross-domain validation is also necessary. The comparison between the Maya and Slovak datasets already showed that results depend strongly on data domain, object morphology, and annotation type. Testing the workflow on additional archaeological contexts, terrain types, and label styles would help determine which findings are specific to the datasets used in this thesis and which conclusions can be generalized to other \gls{lidar}-based archaeological prospection tasks.

A final direction is the integration of model outputs into a human-in-the-loop archaeological workflow. Since the predictions are most useful as candidate layers, future work should focus on how \gls{probabilitymap}, binary masks, object candidates, uncertainty indicators, and \gls{gis}-based editing can be combined in expert review. Expert corrections could then be reused in later training iterations, gradually improving both the reference dataset and the model. Future research may also compare raster-based segmentation with approaches using original \gls{lidar} point cloud or additional terrain derivatives, which was outside the scope of this thesis.

In conclusion, the thesis demonstrated that deep learning can support archaeological interpretation of airborne \gls{lidar}-derived data, but only within a carefully controlled and expert-guided workflow. The developed workflow improved the detection completeness of Maya structures and showed limited but practically meaningful candidate localization for Slovak terrace. The results confirm that \gls{semanticsegmentation} is not a fully automatic archaeological mapping solution in this context. Its value lies in producing reproducible candidate layers, reducing the amount of terrain requiring manual inspection, and clarifying which methodological decisions influence the reliability and usefulness of the final predictions.
